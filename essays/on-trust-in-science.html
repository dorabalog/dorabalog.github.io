On Trust in Science

Theo Baker v Marc Tessier-Lavigne: The Fall of the 11th President of Stanford University

Last year news broke that the president of Stanford University will step down from his role of leading the institution after a series of misconduct allegations regarding twelve of his publications came to light. Marc Tessier-Lavigne, a professor of neuroscience and an expert in Alzheimer’s research, addressed the Stanford community in an open letter about his resignation, where he denied any purposeful personal misconduct while admitting to fraudulent behavior of the personnel in his labs. The misconduct allegations caught on fire when Theo Baker, a freshman at Stanford and a journalist for the Stanford Daily, published a series of articles surrounding the controversy of Dr. Tessier-Lavigne’s work. For his work, Baker became the youngest recipient of the prestigious George Polk Award in investigative journalism, while Tessier-Lavigne had begun retracting his publications, painfully and one by one. Although Tessier-Lavigne resigned from his position of leading the university, and his name (as of this year) appears only in retraction notes when searching in scientific search engines, he still holds his position as principal investigator and a tenured professor of biology.

When Baker started his investigation, he reached out to scientific integrity consultant Elizabeth Bik to verify his concern regarding the potentially photoshopped images. Although there have been whispers in the neuroscience community about the possibility of manipulated results in Tessier-Lavigne’s papers, no one was stepping forward to verify this, that until Bik. Even at the time of Baker writing his first series of articles, Elizabeth was the only person willing to go on record with her analysis. This imposes an important question of how power, legal pressure, and expectations from the scientific community, the government, and/or shareholders to get results, faster and more often affect the integrity of science itself.

Growing pressure in academia has been an issue for quite some time. One way institutions have been able to quantify their success is in their rankings. Multiple factors determine where universities end up on these reports, among which are citation impact and student-to-staff ratio, however, the major drivers are the funding sources and consequently, the revenue in patents and the influence on the community research has generated. This creates an unhealthy and competitive environment where the focus shifts from hard science to a rat race to be the first one to get (any) results out. Recently, a growing number of universities have been trying to counter the pressure by retracting from the ranking system. The latest to do so is the University of Zurich with the explanation that they want to prioritize research quality over quantity. The decision to not engage in competitive spheres of science could be one of the solutions to lighten the heavy burden of scientists who due to time constraints might resort to shortcuts.

As in the Tessier-Lavigne scandal, the most common plagiarism approach is the falsification of figures. Image manipulation techniques have been implemented by hand and are easier to see to the (expert) eye. The question now is: how will result manipulation and misconduct translate into a new era where generative AI can create images just by typing in a prompt? Will techniques for detecting fraud manage to keep up with the evolution of seemingly endless ways to cut corners? Holden Thorp, Editor-in-Chief of Science, certainly thinks so. He announced that the Science family of journals will start using artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools to keep up with image-related plagiarism.

Even though Marc Tessier-Lavigne's actions have eroded public confidence in science, there are still efforts to make science an open and honest space. With new educational approaches (see the content of this course) and public conversations about the topic, hopefully, we will soon be able to experience only genuine mistakes regarding errors in reports.

Written by Dora Balog for EK 800, Responsible Conduct of Research, Boston University, 2024.
