On Tracking Mental Health

Translating Neural Subprocess Detection to Wearable Mental-Health Monitoring: A Licensing Path for Algorithmic Depression Biomarkers

Abstract

Wearable devices, such as smartwatches and rings, have become ubiquitous for tracking heart health, sleep, menstrual cycles, oxygenation, and activity in daily life. These devices, however, have yet to venture into the realm of mental health. At the same time, neurotechnology has seen rapid growth in research and commercial interest. However, safety concerns, data quality limitations, and challenges in decoding neural signals have slowed their translation into a widely accepted class of products. This white paper will attempt to reconcile these two separate paths of development through an examination of recent progress, technical and regulatory challenges, and prospects for wearable mental-health technologies. I will also reflect on how my own research, which focuses on building statistical models for analyzing electrophysiological brain signals, could establish a biomarker for detecting increased depression responses. We will explore how this algorithm would evolve while being developed into an industry product, identify likely pitfalls in commercialization, and discuss ethical and AI-related issues that need to be addressed as the technology scales.

Introduction and motivation

Several years ago, I stumbled upon a headline that impacted the way I view recent advancements in neurotechnology. The article, written by Bloomberg columnist Parmy Olson, had a fairly catchy title: “Brain tech is here, and it is not as creepy as you think” [1]. In the article, Parmy details the advancements in neural implants for those with treatment-resistant depression. Although a well-written and optimistic article, it left one rather pertinent question unanswered: why do we assess mental health so infrequently? Parmy describes how, after implantation, the patient’s health was scored on the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale. These assessments were conducted in clinical settings, however only conducted every two weeks. In the era of various health monitoring systems, it seems rather counterintuitive to wait so long to gather information about an already diagnosed patient’s well-being. Additionally, since the COVID pandemic, great efforts have been made to reduce inpatient visits where possible [2], which raises another question: why are we not monitoring our mental well-being at home? With depression cases soaring in young adults and adolescents [3], it is more important than ever to track this mental illness, the same way we analyze data describing our physical health. This article explores a potential solution, through an algorithmic biomarker - seamlessly integrated into your health tracker of choice - which could detect when a patient’s mental health starts deteriorating, enabling a faster detection of symptom onset, as well as aid in diagnosis when seen by a healthcare professional.

Proposed Solution

The current landscape of wearables consists of a variety of health-tracking devices, mainly using photoplethysmography (PPG), temperature, and/or EEG sensors to provide insight into sleep, fitness, menstrual cycle, and other daily activities. These signals directly (or indirectly) provide information about neural activity and could be useful for developing algorithms that track biomarkers depicting one’s mental health. These categories of data provide two alternate paths of development for marketable products.

In the direct implementation, EEG signals can be used to detect the biomarker indicating rising depression symptoms, while an indirect solution could be developed after adapting the algorithm to utilize proxy signals (PPG, heart rate variability, respiration, etc.). This section will focus on the science behind the direct implementation using EEG, as proxy signals -although more commonly measured by current health trackers- are outside my current scope of expertise and are being explored by other scientific groups focused on brain-heart coupling [4].

EEG signals are collections of voltage measurements as a result of activity in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Since EEG is a surface-level signal, one can treat an EEG signal as the weighted sum of neural activity underneath the electrode, as a function of depth. The resulting signal is a one-dimensional time trace, so we must implement various statistical modeling approaches to discern which subprocesses comprise the DNA of the recorded signal. In the simplest analysis, the goal is to observe the ratio of inhibitory to excitatory activation as two subprocesses contributing to the surface signal, with some signals adding and some canceling each other out by nature [5]. The changes in this ratio of excitatory to inhibitory (E/I) activity is a biomarker that can be measured as an indicator for various underlying conditions.

The power spectrum of an EEG signal in this context has two components: the first describes one (or multiple) peaks at specific frequencies (which we denote as oscillations and are usually connected to a specific brain state), and the second captures the overall background (aperiodic) activity. Historically, research has mainly focused on elucidating the functional role of oscillations, while largely ignoring the background activity (despite the fact that these signals have nonzero power across the entire spectrum - hence containing encrypted information!) [6]. In recent years, efforts have been made to develop algorithms focused on deconvolving the two components, again in hopes of a more objective measurement of the oscillations [7]. Notably for our purposes, this work has led to the discovery that changes in the aperiodic part of the power spectrum do occur, which have begun to gain focus in research, being tested against multiple mental health disorders in clinical settings [8]. One of the most promising studies connecting disorders to a change in this aperiodic part of the power spectrum explored symptoms of depression, indicated by increased excitatory activity in individuals as their symptoms increased.

My research group focuses on developing new methods for understanding the underlying subprocesses [9] and has a more accurate decomposition of the power spectrum than the current leading method. With this improved methodology, we could more precisely detect changes in the E/I ratio and flag them as depression symptom onsets. The advantage of this solution lies in the small amounts of data necessary to compute the power spectra - the sampling frequency of any modern health wearable is sufficient, as is the spatial resolution (number of electrodes) available in consumer-grade EEG devices [10] (proxy devices have comparable numbers). Using this algorithmic setup, plans for data collection, validation, and IP filings can be established, after which collaboration with wearable companies can start, as detailed in the next section.

Business Assessment

The US wearable market has a well-established customer base; recently, as many as one in three adults are reported to wear a health-monitoring device [11]. This comes with both advantages and disadvantages for emerging technologies. Offering a software product is advantageous to developers due to lower capital risk, and is further augmented by the reduced time it takes to push the solution from conception to market. In this scenario, the time window for development would need to account for integration and testing in a wearable hardware device, which may be unpredictable. A reasonable approach would be to first adapt the technology for hardware directly measuring EEG, and later expand to devices measuring indirect signals such as PPG (smartwatches, bracelets, rings). This staggered approach would help to avoid potential licensing conflicts and secure a dual stream of income, since the underlying technologies of the devices utilize different core measurements

The primary costs of bringing this solution to customers lie in: investments in research and development of the product (including, but not limited to, data collection and validation), filing IPs, and integration engineering once partnered with a hardware company. The proof of concept stage of the timeline can be limited to as little as 6 to 12 months before partnering with wearable producers. Based on this, the first year of development would be kept to a lean $200K, backed by seed funding sources, which would include ~$50K for a limited scale data collection and validation study (including participant payments, personnel and equipment), ~$5K for provisional IP filing(s) and the proof of concept model development, and most of the cost would be dedicated to personnel conducting the research/engineering, approximately $100-145K. After year 1, we can expand to preparing a production model with our partners, costing approximately another $200K, after accounting for engineering, integration, QA, and documentation labor. The last stage would focus on integrating the solution into the hardware. This process is expected to be around $85K, with ~$25K for yearly maintenance.

To offset production costs, revenue will be collected via licensing fees, with an upfront purchase of $100K and a royalty of $0.75/device. We expect to offset the first year with seed funds and subsequent years through additional rounds of funding, supplemented through our collaborations with partners. The royalty and licensing fees will then be reinvested into research and development needed for the next hardware partner and their device.

Public Policy Issues

Since the business solution consists of software analyzing consumer health data, keeping privacy, security, and the accuracy of performance claims will be of utmost importance. The partnership with wellness devices (rather than medical devices) will lower regulatory requirements, as well as hardware safety and certification requirements on our side. On the other hand, a software solution can pose a risk as the algorithm will have to demonstrate effective performance relatively quickly, in addition to the development items discussed in the previous section. Joint legal obligations between partners would consist of filing for SaMD clearance, postmarket surveillance of the algorithm, and legal responsibility for claims and liability, all to be discussed when signing the licensing agreement.

While collecting the dataset, all medical consumer acts (ex. HIPAA) must be considered in the protocols, and participants will be able to read upon signing how their data will be used in the study. Standard data practices for health-data studies will be implemented, including the highest levels of data encryption and storage of de-identified data in a secure location. If the product were to expand to other continents, we would modify the protocols accordingly to comply with additional consumer laws (such as the European Union GDPR).

Although this is not an AI algorithm, AI integration is possible in various parts of the development process. In the name of transparency, there must be a strict process outlining all steps that use publicly available AI agents. At this time, this LLM integration would be limited to creating code, as well as summarizing and overseeing reports, meetings, and other documentation.

Conclusion

Neurotechnology and wearable devices have come a long way since their conception. Today’s challenges focus on solving the integration of these two worlds, which includes finding ways to aid people in tracking their mental health as closely as their physical health. Ever since reading the Bloomberg article, discussed at the beginning of this paper, there has been a shift in how we view neurotechnologies and how prevalent wearable technologies are in our everyday lives. As an example, Oura doubled its $5.2 billion valuation from December of 2024 to $11.2 billion in October of 2025 [12], indicating the growing demand for wearable health technologies.

Unfortunately, high interest in this field comes with a downside. Some companies, while chasing the titles of ‘first to accomplish’, resort to unsafe practices in hopes of reducing the time needed to get products to the market. One such example is the work conducted by Neuralink, which reported animal abuse [13], employee mistreatment [14], and woefully unsafe designs [15], which were revealed as a side effect of their high-profile case of implanting a brain-computer interface [16]. Although our algorithmic solution does not propose any medical intervention, ethical questions coming from various subareas of the field must be considered to raise awareness and safely integrate any kind of device into daily life.
